#Patients pay the price as Michigan healthcare systems consolidate

Michigan hospital systems are getting bigger, but have patients paid a price for consolidation? This story will focus on the shrinking number of players in Michigan healthcare and how it has affected access to specialty care throughout the state.

300,000 residents nearly lost their access to the state’s premier health system thanks to an insurance dispute in June, healthcare costs continue to soar and a $2.2 billion hospital construction project just reached 20 stories tall last month: it’s time explain the juxtaposition between shiny buildings and growing systems with patient struggles.

5/31: The Idea

Last week, Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan finally reached a settlement with the University of Michigan Health (Michigan Medicine). The contract renegotiation between the two systems, which allows patients under the insurer to receive care at Michigan Medicine, had nearly failed, threatening 300,000 residents with a loss of care from one of the state’s most reputable health systems. As in many industries, consolidation has taken over healthcare. A 2022 Michigan health care merger created a combined 22-hospital system, Corewell Health, with claims of improving patient care. In 2023, Michigan Medicine acquired another health system as well. Still, insurance companies blame hospitals for rising costs, and insurance companies blame hospitals. This April, Michigan Medicine sang praise for the results of the 2023 merger.

But a May KFF study showed hospital prices are rising much faster for private insurance than Medicare since 2019. Michigan Medicine claims it should be rewarded with better contracts thanks to its quality of care and focus on specialty medicine. But how has consolidation really worked out for patients, from their health to their pocketbooks? Especially as Detroit’s hospital buildings get shinier (see Henry Ford Hospital’s recent $2.2 billion expansion).

Enter: Health Resources and Services Administration data. The HRSA’s Area Health Resource files provide a wealth of information on hospitals by county, state and for the entire nation. An initial look reveals that in just one year, hospital expenditures per non-general and long term hospital in Washtenaw County (includes Ann Arbor, where Michigan Health is based) grew nearly $30 million to reach over $1 billion per hospital as of the most recent data available from 2023 (we have a data lag here according to the documentation). Why is that so much larger than Illinois’ largest county for the same measure, at about $600 million? How quickly have these expenditures risen, county-by-county? Is Michigan rising faster than other states, and how do increases show in areas with newly-merged hospitals? Have the mergers good for expenditures? And patients, such as with care quality metrics also in the data?
