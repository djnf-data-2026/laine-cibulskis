Michigan hospital systems are getting bigger, but have patients paid a price for consolidation? This story will focus on the shrinking number of players in Michigan healthcare and how it has affected access to specialty care throughout the state. One example: a 2022 Michigan health care merger created a combined 22-hospital system, Corewell Health, with claims of improving patient care.

This analysis focuses on the before and after effects of the Corewell merger on counties affected by the merger (those that have Corewell hospitals). Another distinction was also made to test health disparities between northern and southern Michigan.

Last week, Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan finally reached a settlement with the University of Michigan Health (Michigan Medicine). The contract renegotiation between the two systems, which allows patients under the insurer to receive care at Michigan Medicine, had nearly failed, threatening 300,000 Detroit residents with a loss of care from one of the state’s most reputable health systems. As in many industries, consolidation has taken over healthcare.

In 2023, Michigan Medicine acquired another health system as well. Still, insurance companies blame hospitals for rising costs, and insurance companies blame hospitals. This April, Michigan Medicine sang praise for the results of the 2023 merger.

But a May KFF study showed hospital prices are rising much faster for private insurance than Medicare since 2019. Another KFF briefing from 2024 explains that a large body of evidence points to healthcare consolidation increasing costs, but that the verdict on patient outcomes is less straightforward.

Michigan Medicine claims it should be rewarded with better contracts thanks to its quality of care and focus on specialty medicine. But how has consolidation really worked out for patients in Michigan, from their health to their pocketbooks? Especially as Detroit’s hospital buildings get shinier and taller, as with Henry Ford Hospital’s in-progress $2.2 billion, 20-story expansion?

Thanks to an insurance dispute, nearly 300,000 patients lost their access to the state’s premier health system thanks to an insurance dispute in June, healthcare costs continue to soar and a $2.2 billion hospital construction project just reached 20 stories tall last month: it’s time explain the juxtaposition between shiny buildings and growing systems with patient struggles.

Only Michigan’s specialty care is concentrated in the populous Southwest area of the state: 16 of Michigan’s 83 counties reported having hospitals providing specialty care. One key note for Michigan: that care is concentrated in the south of the state, as opposed to states with more equally distributed healthcare centers like Illinois, which has a similar overall population (Chicago and UIUC in southern Illinois are two major health centers). They’re very populated Michigan counties, but the spending disparity is clear, especially as more investment is brought to Detroit.

Michiganders need to know how consolidation has actually affected their access to healthcare, beyond what the pressers from healthcare companies and daily news can tell them. Additionally, the Detroit News has identified a gap in its data coverage of healthcare, in addition to a gap in its business of healthcare coverage, which is usually covered on an as-needed basis.

Business context: Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan

According to BCBS of Michigan’s financial statements, published on their website, premium revenue increased by over $1 billion from 2024 to 2025. However, financial weakness shows in a consistent operating loss, which was at a $975 million loss in 2025. The company insures over 4 million Michiganders.

Contextualizing the increase and possible holes

The final story would include data over time for all specialty hospitals by county, not just those reporting expenditures. Additionally, individual-hospital level data, that was noted above, will be used to pinpoint areas with mergers and hospital closures.

The story cannot claim causality, but it can show just how concentrated hospital spending is in just a few counties, and how that has changed over time as consolidation has increased. Incorporating the individual-hospital level data will help.

How common is it for other states to have specialty care concentrated in just a few areas? What about the differences in states by county-level highs? What is the best way to connect this expenditure data with patient cost data? And what will tracking hospital locations and expenditures over a longer time period show when considering larger merger dates?

Short-term general hospitals Hospitals that provide non-specialized care, and the majority of their patients stay for fewer than 30 days.

Short-term non-general hospitals Short term non-general hospitals provide specialized care, and the majority of their patients stay for fewer than 30 days.

Long-term hospitals Long-term hospitals may provide either non specialized or specialized care, and the majority of their patients stay for 30 or more days.

Preventable Hospital Stays Rate Hospitalization for ambulatory care-sensitive conditions, diagnoses usually treatable in outpatient services, suggests that the quality of care provided in the outpatient setting was not accessible. The measure may also represent a tendency to overuse emergency room and urgent care as a main source of care.
